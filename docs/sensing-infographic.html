How the sensor works electrically

Nothing electrical ever touches the water. The level is measured as tiny changes in capacitance through a 1.2 mm plastic wall, and the temperature is read by a digital sensor through a thinner 0.8 mm window.

Level sensing

Each electrode and the water form a capacitor

The copper pad is one plate, the plastic wall is the dielectric, and the water is the other plate. Water with a little dissolved salt behaves as a conductor at the sensing frequency, so a pad with water in front of it gains a capacitor to ground.

Inside the ESP32-S3

Measuring a capacitor by timing it

The touch peripheral charges and discharges each pad with a fixed current, flipping direction every time the pad voltage crosses an upper or lower threshold. A larger capacitance charges more slowly, so fewer cycles fit in the same time. The cycle count, or equivalently the time per cycle, is the raw reading.

One channel is measured at a time. The other pads are left floating (high-impedance), which, as the water-film study showed, stops a wet film on the wall from making dry pads look wet.

Waveforms are illustrative. Real readings also include about 10–20 pF of pin and trace capacitance, which the firmware subtracts as a baseline.

The shield

Driving the shield with the same signal makes its capacitance disappear

A hatched copper plane sits 0.2 mm behind every electrode. Grounded, it would be a 40 pF capacitor that swamps the 5.8 pF from the water. The ESP32-S3's shield output drives it with a copy of the measuring waveform, so pad and shield always sit at the same voltage, no current flows between them, and that capacitance drops out of the reading.

The shield also blocks the battery and electronics behind the board. The pads only "see" forward, through the wall.

Try it

Six pads turn into a continuous level

Each pad's reading rises in a straight line as the water climbs its 9 mm height. The firmware divides each pad's change by its own fully-wet value, adds the fractions up, and multiplies by the 10 mm pitch. The result is continuous to about ±1 mm, not just six steps.

Temperature

A digital thermometer behind a thin window

The TMP117 measures its own silicon temperature with an on-chip bandgap reference and reports it digitally over I²C, so there's no analog signal to drift. Heat reaches it through the 0.8 mm thinned wall, a soft gap pad, a copper patch on the front of the board and a row of vias to the chip's exposed pad.
