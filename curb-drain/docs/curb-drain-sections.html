Shower entry curb · fixed glass panel · JLC3DP MJF PA12 · final design

Curb drain clip, in section

The final design: a 3 ft drain strip along the shower side of a flat granite curb, printed as three sections that lock together with dovetails. Water runs down the fixed glass panel, collects against the silicone bead, enters the strip at the comb, runs along capillary grooves (13 for every 26 mm), over the edge, and drips onto the tile. All drawings are to scale from the files you upload to JLC.

The whole run, from above

Three 304.8 mm sections cover 914 mm of curb. Each one drains on its own, because the grooves run across the curb rather than along it, so no water has to cross a joint. The pale band along each section is the comb inlet, which sits in the puddle just short of the silicone bead.

Side section through the curb

This is the same at every point along the run. Nothing is exaggerated: at 2.6 mm the strip is barely thicker than the puddle it sits in. Red dash-dot lines mark where sections A and C are cut.

AGrooved arm, cut across the curb

The grooves are V-shaped so their corners pull water along by capillary action. Their floors sit 1.3 mm above the stone, below the 1.6 mm depth the puddle thins to, so the water stays connected to them.

BOne groove, and how water sits in it

Drawn 150× actual size. Gravity on the leg pulls the water, and the meniscus in each groove responds by curving. The more it curves, the harder it pulls, until it recedes into the bottom of the V. The flow model was solved for each of these shapes.

The rule that decides it: water creeps along the groove by itself only if its contact angle θ with the nylon is below 90° − α = 69°. Fresh nylon can be above that. A wash with dish soap brings it well below.

CComb inlet by the silicone bead

For the last 8 mm of the arm, each groove becomes a slot cut right through to the granite. Water enters at stone level, so the clip keeps draining after the puddle is thinner than the grooves are high.

DDrip edge, seen from inside the shower

The leg's grooves carry the water down. The bottom edge is a sawtooth, 13 teeth per section, so the water gathers at each point and drops cleanly onto the tile instead of creeping back under the strip.

Why the arm has to sit on the stone

Heights in mm. The puddle thins to about 1.6 mm before it starts to shrink. A millimetre of tape sounds harmless, but it lifts every groove out of reach. Tape the vertical leg to the curb's inner face and let the arm lie directly on the granite.

EJoint between sections, from above

A dovetail tab on the arm of each section drops into a socket in the next one from above. It can't pull apart along the curb, and any section lifts straight out for cleaning. There are no flexing snaps to break.

The fit has 0.3 mm of clearance at the sides, inside JLC's 0.2–0.4 mm range for parts that fit together, and 0.5 mm of slack at the end so the nylon can expand when it's warm and damp.

Each section is accurate to about ±1.2 mm in length (±0.4%), so leave a few millimetres at the ends of the run.

Method: Stokes flow solved on each groove cross-section (checked against exact pipe solutions to 0.3%), coupled to a capillary and gravity flow model along the arm, bend and leg, and a draining-puddle model. Water at 30 °C. Results are for one 30 mm width of strip; the 3 ft run has 30 times the capacity and keeps every part of the puddle in touch with it. Contact angles on the nylon and granite were not measured, so the results cover a range. Fresh, unwashed nylon may bead water; wash the strip with dish soap before use.
